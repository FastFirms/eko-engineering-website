Water Services · Demand Management

Short-term demand management, Adelaide River, NT

A 20% reduction in peak daily consumption — deferring supply-side augmentation, faster and at a fraction of the cost.

Client: Power and Water CorporationCategory: Water Services · Demand ManagementLocation: Adelaide River, Northern TerritoryProject value: Not disclosedEngagement period: 2018–2020

The client

Power and Water Corporation (P&W) is the Northern Territory Government-owned utility responsible for water, sewerage, and electricity services across the NT, including to remote and regional communities. The Adelaide River township sits roughly 110 kilometres south of Darwin on the Stuart Highway, at the edge of the Top End's wet-dry tropics — a region defined by one of the most dramatic seasonal rainfall patterns on the continent and the acute supply pressures that accompany them. Despite its proximity to Darwin, Adelaide River operates as a largely self-contained supply system, drawing groundwater treated through a biological filtration plant commissioned in 2015 to address elevated iron and manganese. That single-source dependency leaves the township acutely exposed to seasonal demand spikes, with no neighbouring network to draw from when the margin narrows.

P&W engaged Eko to deliver a targeted demand management response to peak-season water stress. The requirement was not simply to reduce consumption but to do so accurately, rapidly, and with minimal disruption to the community. The central question was whether a data-first approach could achieve the same supply-security outcome as capital augmentation, at a fraction of the cost and in a fraction of the time.

Why it matters

Water supply in remote and regional NT amplifies the consequences of demand management failure. Seasonal demand in the Top End is not a gradual curve — it is a steep spike driven by population movement, agricultural activity, and the absence of dry-season rainfall recharge. Single-town supply systems have limited buffer: no interconnected network, no emergency draw from a neighbouring grid, no rapid augmentation that arrives within weeks.

The default institutional response to that pressure is capital investment in new or augmented infrastructure — slow to procure, slow to construct, and expensive. What Adelaide River needed was a response that worked within the season the problem existed. That requires a different starting point: not where the pipes are, but where the consumption is.

The problem

During peak periods, Adelaide River's supply was coming under increasing pressure. Consumption was spiking in patterns the system had not been designed to absorb. P&W held historical consumption data, but it had not been interrogated at the resolution needed to understand which properties were driving the peaks, by how much, or when. Without that picture, any intervention would be blunt — reduce broadly and hope the right properties were captured.

The deeper issue was the absence of a reliable consumption map. Billing records, network data, and field observations sat in separate systems and separate conversations. They had never been assembled into a working picture of actual demand by property, by period, by peak.

You cannot manage what you cannot measure. The consumption data existed. The analysis did not. That gap was where the supply risk lived.

Without a reliable baseline, demand management defaults to guesswork. Broad messaging reaches everyone equally and therefore reaches the highest users least effectively. The cost of not building the consumption picture first is not just financial — it is the cost of interventions that don't work, and the supply risk that accumulates while they're being tried.

Our approach

  • Conducted rapid analysis of historical consumption data across the network to identify the highest-using properties — by volume, by timing, and relative to the network average. This came before any field activity, so resources could be concentrated where they'd make the most difference.
  • Deployed data loggers on high-use properties to confirm and refine the desktop consumption profiles, adding the temporal resolution needed to understand demand shape — peaks by hour, by day, across the full period.
  • Led community and stakeholder consultation to drive voluntary reduction, targeted at the highest-use properties with evidence-based conversations rather than broadcast messaging.
  • Tracked outcomes against the 20% peak-daily-reduction target in real time using the logger network — the target governed the program throughout, rather than being assessed at the end.

The result

The engagement delivered a 20% reduction in peak daily consumption across the Adelaide River network. Supply-side augmentation was deferred. A rapid, data-driven demand management approach produced the supply-security outcome that would otherwise have required capital works — faster, at substantially lower cost, and within the season in which the problem existed.

For a regional NT township on a single-source system, a 20% reduction in peak daily consumption is the difference between a system operating within its design parameters and one operating at or beyond its limit. The significance lies not just in the number but in what it demonstrates: when consumption data is properly interrogated and field deployment is targeted by that analysis, demand management can substitute for infrastructure investment in exactly the situations where infrastructure is slowest and most expensive to deliver.

What it means to you

The consumption data is already in your network. What's missing is the analysis that turns it into a decision.

You manage water in a system that does not forgive seasonal surprises. Whether you're a sustainability officer defending the quarterly numbers, a facilities manager watching the meter run while the cooling tower log stays empty, or an operations lead quietly absorbing bill shock nobody upstairs has fully understood — you're the person whose name is on the plan. And you're working with data that exists in abundance but has never been assembled into the one thing you actually need: a map of who is consuming what, when, and by how much relative to everything else.

The frustrating part isn't that the data is missing. It's that the data has always been there — billing records, meter reads, SCADA outputs, field notes from technicians who've been telling you for years where the trouble spots are. It lives in separate systems and separate conversations. So when the pressure arrives, you're working from an average, not a map. You know the network is stressed. You can't say, with precision, where to push first.

Before Eko deployed a single data logger in Adelaide River, a desktop analysis had already identified the properties driving peak demand — by volume, by timing, and by their weight against the network average. That sequence matters. The field resources that followed went to the right addresses, not the most accessible ones. The 20% reduction wasn't achieved by doing more of what had already been tried. It was achieved by doing the analysis first.
